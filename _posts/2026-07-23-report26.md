In mid-July 2026, Nuffield College hosted the fourth annual Oxford summer school on Large Language Models for Social Science, supported by Nuffield College and the Centre for Advanced Social Science Methods, with research support from Nebius. Over five days, early-career researchers worked through lectures from practitioners building language models in industry, research talks from social scientists using them, and a week-long collaborative research competition.

## What we taught

The technical programme this year was taught almost entirely by working practitioners. Tatiana Shavrina (Meta) covered LLM agents and evaluation; Sagi Shaier (JGU Mainz) taught continual learning and why models forget; Ilya Boytsov (Nebius) covered efficient models and compression; Piotr Mazurek (Liquid.AI) taught the economics of inference; Sergei Skvortsov (Nebius) covered cloud and self-hosted deployment; and Ibragim Badertdinov (Nebius) taught LLM data and reinforcement-learning environments.

Research talks came from Margaret Roberts (UC San Diego), Roberto-Rafael Maura-Rivero (Meta and Oxford), Christopher Barrie (NYU), Andreu Casas (Oxford), and Elliott Ash (ETH Zurich), each connecting the technical sessions to live empirical work.

Slides and notebooks are published on the [materials page](https://llmsforsocialscience.net) and the [GitHub organisation](https://github.com/Oxford-LLMs-Research).

## The AI Respondents Challenge

The biggest change from previous years: the collaborative project ran the whole week rather than the final two days. Real people answered the World Values Survey, we hid some of their answers, and six teams built pipelines to predict what each person said, disclosing the respondent attributes and prompts behind every prediction.

We scored it as an expanding circle of difficulty. Teams were first tested on held-out respondents, then on countries that never appeared in training, then on four target questions released mid-week with no warning, and finally, on Thursday morning with the identity of the data kept secret until then, on two entirely different surveys: European Social Survey wave 11 and Latinobarómetro 2023, predicted zero-shot.

The result is the most interesting thing to come out of the week. Every team beat majority guessing comfortably in-domain, and moving to unseen countries cost them almost nothing. Moving to a different survey instrument cost them most of their skill. And on Latinobarómetro questions in countries the World Values Survey has never covered, every team scored *worse* than guessing. Whatever these models have learned about how people answer survey questions travels well within a European frame and poorly out of it.

The [final leaderboard](https://llmsforsocialscience.net/research/challenge/) is public, along with the [dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/oxford-llms/ai-respondents-challenge) and [starter code](https://github.com/Oxford-LLMs-Research/ai-respondents-challenge). A paper drawn from the challenge is being drafted with 2026 speakers and participants as co-authors, the third cohort in a row to go from summer school to co-authorship.

## Beyond the lecture room

An opening social on Monday evening and a guided walking tour of Oxford on Wednesday gave participants and speakers time together away from the screens. As in previous years, several of the week's most useful conversations happened on the walk.

## Looking ahead

Participants told us plainly what to fix: balance the research teams by technical experience rather than a coarse survey, release preparatory materials well in advance, decide admissions earlier so travel is affordable, and automate the challenge scoring. All four are on the list for 2027.

If you are an early-career researcher who wants to understand language models properly and work on a real research problem with them, watch this space for the 2027 application.
