## The Derivative You Already Use

Start with an object from your own toolkit. In a linear regression, the coefficient on
income answers a sensitivity question: <em>if income rises by one unit, how much does the
outcome move?</em> You have interpreted thousands of these. A derivative is exactly that
number, generalised: for any function, at any point,

</div>

For a linear model the sensitivity is the same everywhere — that is what "linear" means, and
why a regression table can print one number per variable. For a nonlinear function the
sensitivity is <em>local</em>: it holds at the point you asked about, for small nudges, and
elsewhere it differs. That locality is not a defect; it is the working mode of everything
that follows:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.linearApprox} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.linearApproxCaption} />
</div>

One framing is deliberately avoided here: <em>the derivative as the slope of a tangent line
on a curve</em>. It is not wrong — it is the picture for one input and one output — but it
does not survive the trip to a loss function over four thousand million parameters, where
there is no curve to draw and no tangent to see. Sensitivity survives: "nudge this, how much
does that move?" is askable in any number of dimensions, which is why it is the framing this
track builds on.

## Partial Derivatives and the Gradient

Real functions take many inputs — a loss takes millions. The move that tames them is
embarrassingly simple: <strong>nudge one input, hold all the others still.</strong> The
resulting one-at-a-time sensitivity is a partial derivative, and computing one uses no new
mathematics — every other variable is treated as a constant, and the rules you just revised
apply. Collect all the partials and you have the gradient:

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Gradient</p>
  <Tex tex={math.gradDef} />
</div>

That last clause — <em>points in the direction of steepest ascent</em> — is the fact all of
M6 rests on, so it deserves an argument rather than an assertion. Suppose you take a small
unit-length step in some direction <strong>u</strong>. Each coordinate of the step buys you
that coordinate's partial's worth of change, and the total is a sum of products — which M1
taught you to recognise on sight:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.dirDeriv} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.dirDerivCaption} />
</div>

The rate of change in direction <strong>u</strong> is a <em>dot product</em> with the
gradient, and a dot product with a unit vector is maximised by pointing it along the other
vector — that was M1's geometric reading, verbatim. So the gradient points uphill because
alignment maximises dot products; there is nothing more to it. The same line also reads
downhill: step <em>against</em> the gradient and the dot product is as negative as possible,
which is the entire idea M6 turns into gradient descent.

## The Chain Rule: Rates Compose by Multiplication

Models are compositions — a linear layer feeds a nonlinearity feeds another layer feeds a
loss. The chain rule is the fact that makes compositions differentiable, and it is best met
as something you already believe about conversion rates. If one euro buys 1.1 dollars, and
one dollar buys 150 yen, then one euro buys 165 yen: <em>rates through a chain multiply.</em>

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.chainRule} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.chainRuleCaption} />
</div>

Each factor is <em>local</em> — the sensitivity of one link to its immediate input, knowing
nothing about the rest of the chain — and the end-to-end sensitivity is their product. That
locality is what will make the whole machinery scale: no link ever needs the global picture.

The multiplication also carries a warning that becomes a major theme two tracks from now.
Multiply ten factors of 0.5 and the product is about 0.001; ten factors of 2 and it is a
thousand. <strong>Long chains drive sensitivities to extremes</strong> unless the local rates
sit near one — small signals die before reaching the far end, large ones blow up. Problem 5
makes you compute it; D7 shows it killing recurrent networks on long sequences, and the
architecture that fixed it — attention — is where this curriculum has been heading all along.
The seed is planted here: it is nothing deeper than repeated multiplication.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    The chain rule is conversion rates multiplying: end-to-end sensitivity is the product of
    local sensitivities, each computed knowing nothing beyond its own link. Products of many
    factors run to extremes — the vanishing and exploding gradients of D7, three modules early.
  </p>
</div>

## Computational Graphs

Now the structural step that turns two small facts into the engine of deep learning. Any
expression can be drawn as a <strong>graph</strong>: inputs enter, each node applies one
elementary operation, and the output emerges. Draw it, and the chain rule stops being a
formula and becomes traffic: sensitivities flow backwards along the edges, each edge
multiplying by its local rate. Step through it:

<ChainRuleStepper />

Two things to notice while stepping — they are the module's punchline. First, <strong>every
backward step is identical in form</strong>: what arrived from downstream, times the local
derivative of this one node. The square node contributes 2r; the add node copies; the
multiply node hands each input the <em>other</em> input as its rate. No step consults the
global expression. Second, when paths branch, contributions <strong>add</strong> — the full
rule for any graph:

  <Tex tex={math.pathsRule} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.pathsRuleCaption} />

Read that caption again, because it is not a preview but the thing itself: this
product-along-paths, sum-over-paths procedure <em>is</em> backpropagation. What D4 adds is an
efficiency observation — sweeping the graph once from right to left computes every node's
sensitivity while reusing shared path segments, instead of re-multiplying each path from
scratch. The mathematics is complete on this page; the deep-learning track will only make the
graph bigger.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    Any expression is a graph; the sensitivity of the output to any node is the product of
    local derivatives along each connecting path, summed over paths. Every step is local —
    arrived times local rate — and that mechanicalness is why a machine can differentiate a
    billion-parameter model without cleverness.
  </p>
</div>

The reasoning is problem 6's, and it is worth internalising before ever writing training
code: a gradient is one sensitivity per adjustable number, so it must have exactly the
parameter's shape. When a framework reports a gradient of a different shape than its
parameter, you do not have a subtle bug — you have a wrong computation, and the shapes just
told you so for free. This is M2's type-system discipline extended to training, and it is
the single most effective sanity check the deep-learning track will offer you.

One honest note: how vector-by-vector derivatives are <em>written down</em> (row versus
column arrangements) is a convention on which textbooks disagree, and silently mixing two
conventions is a classic source of confusion. The appendix states the conventions properly;
this track writes gradients in the shape of the thing varied, which is also what every major
framework returns.

## Where This Lands

Assemble the module. A neural network's loss is one enormous composed expression: linear
layers (M2), nonlinearities, and a final loss node, drawn as a graph with millions of
parameters among its inputs. Backpropagation — the procedure the widget stepped through, with
the paths rule doing the bookkeeping and the matrix-calculus facts supplying the local
rates — delivers <em>every parameter's sensitivity in one backward sweep</em>. Nothing else
is needed: no formula per architecture, no global analysis, no insight at any node beyond
"arrived times local".

That is the module's landing, and it is deliberately anticlimactic. The mystique around
backpropagation dissolves into three sentences you now own: derivatives are sensitivities;
rates compose by multiplication, summing over branches; gradients wear their parameter's
shape. What remains for the deep-learning track is engineering — doing this efficiently, at
scale, and reading the failures (vanishing products, exploding products, dead units) that
this module has already equipped you to predict. And one question is left deliberately open:
knowing every parameter's downhill direction, <em>how far should you step?</em> That number —
the learning rate — is the single knob of M6, and it has failure modes of its own.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a graph, once: every parameter's sensitivity
    from one backward sweep of local multiplications. The mathematics is complete here — the
    deep-learning track adds scale, efficiency, and the reading of its failure modes.
  </p>
</div>
