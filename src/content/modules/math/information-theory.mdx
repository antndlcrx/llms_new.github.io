## Surprise

Start from an instinct you already trust professionally: <strong>rare events are
informative</strong>. A p-value is a measure of surprise; a headline is news precisely when
it was improbable; a survey answer tells you most about a respondent when few others gave
it. Information theory begins by taking that instinct seriously enough to give it units.

So: assign a number — the <em>surprise</em> — to an event, depending only on its
probability. Three requirements, none negotiable. Certainty carries no surprise. Rarer
means more surprising. And when two <em>independent</em> events both happen, their
surprises should <em>add</em> — learning two unrelated facts is exactly as informative as
learning each separately. But independent probabilities <em>multiply</em> (M7). A function
that turns multiplication into addition has one name:

That is the whole derivation, and it is worth pausing on what just happened: the logarithm
was not chosen for convenience — it was <em>forced</em> by wanting surprises to add
(problem 4 has you make the argument airtight). A fair coin flip carries exactly 1 bit. An
event with probability one in a thousand carries about 10. The unit is real, and the next
section makes it physical.

  <p>
    Surprise is −log p — forced, not chosen, by the requirement that independent surprises
    add while independent probabilities multiply. Base 2 names the unit: the bit, the
    surprise of one fair coin flip.
  </p>

## Entropy: Expected Surprise

A distribution produces events, each carrying its own surprise; the distribution's
<strong>entropy</strong> is the surprise you should <em>expect</em> — and it turns out to
be a physical quantity you can watch being spent.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Entropy</p>
  <Tex tex={math.entropyDef} />
</div>

The coding interpretation is what makes entropy more than a formula. Suppose you must
transmit a long stream of symbols, and every bit costs money. A fixed-width code spends the
same on every symbol — 3 bits each, for six symbols — no matter how predictable the stream
is. But if some symbols are common, you can do better: give the frequent symbols short
codewords and the rare ones long ones, and the <em>average</em> falls. How far can it fall?
To the entropy, and no further — that is Shannon's source coding theorem (stated properly
in the appendix). Entropy is the <strong>compression floor</strong>: the bits per symbol the
distribution itself demands, however clever the coder. Watch the bits:

predictable, so nothing can be abbreviated; near-certainty is nearly free — a stream that
is almost all one symbol barely needs saying. On real language the effect is dramatic:

## Cross-Entropy: The Bill for the Wrong Model

Everything so far assumed you know the true distribution. You never do — you have a model.
Cross-entropy prices the gap.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Cross-Entropy</p>
  <Tex tex={math.ceDef} />
</div>

The operational story is the widget's wrong-model mode, and it deserves to be run rather
than read: switch <strong>Build the code for</strong> to a distribution that is not yours,
and the average bits per symbol rises off the floor. Symbols the wrong model considered
rare got long codewords; where they are in fact common, you pay the excess on every
occurrence. Scoring data with the wrong model has a <em>bill</em>, the bill is in bits, and
it is never negative.

Which is why cross-entropy is <strong>the</strong> loss. A language model scores each
actual next token, and the loss averages the model's surprise at what the data did.
Minimising it drives the model toward the data distribution, because cross-entropy bottoms
out exactly at equality. And the payoff M7 set up arrives in one line:

        positive, so a softmax model can disbelieve a token intensely but never rule it out —

    which is why it is the loss; and minimising it is maximum likelihood in different units.
  </p>
</div>

## KL Divergence: The Excess

Cross-entropy bundles two costs: the irreducible floor that even the true model pays, and
the excess charged for being wrong. KL divergence is the excess alone:

In the widget's wrong-model mode this is the labelled gap between the average code length
and the floor — the part of the bill that is your model's fault. It is always non-negative
(the appendix proves it in four lines via Jensen's inequality, and problem 7 asks you to

The asymmetry is not a technical blemish — it is a design surface, and the LLMs track uses
it. When preference tuning (DPO, RLHF) keeps a fine-tuned policy near its reference model
with a KL penalty, the <em>direction</em> of the penalty decides which deviations are
cheap: the standard choice punishes the policy for inventing behaviour the reference finds
improbable, while letting it sharpen toward a subset of what the reference already does — a
leash that permits specialisation and forbids wandering (problem 5 works both directions).
Knowledge distillation makes the complementary choice. When you meet these losses in the
LLMs track, you will know them for what they are: bits of excess surprise, pointed
carefully.

## Perplexity

The last translation. Loss curves are quoted in cross-entropy; papers and leaderboards
quote <strong>perplexity</strong>, and the relationship is one exponential:

  <Tex tex={math.pplDef} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.pplDefCaption} />
</div>

The effective-branching-factor reading is what makes perplexity humane: a cross-entropy of
3.2 bits per token means little to intuition, but "as uncertain as a nine-way choice" is a
picture. Perplexity 1 is omniscience — and, on real text, a red flag that your evaluation
has leaked. Perplexity equal to the vocabulary size is a model that has learned nothing at
all. Real language models live in the low single and double digits on English text, a
number worth sitting with: out of fifty thousand possibilities, context typically narrows
the next token to the practical equivalent of a handful.

One warning with a publication trail behind it: <strong>perplexity is per-token, and
tokens are not a shared unit.</strong> Two models with different tokenizers slice the same
text into different sequences; the model with longer tokens packs more surprise into each
one and reports higher perplexity at identical quality. Comparing them is comparing prices
in different currencies without converting (problem 6 makes it precise; L3 returns to it
when tokenization gets its own module). The convertible currencies are total
log-probability of the same string, or bits per character.

## Where This Lands: Three Derivations, and a Track Complete

The LLMs track uses three information-theoretic facts constantly, and this module has now
derived all of them. The <strong>loss</strong>: cross-entropy between each true next token
and the predicted distribution — expected surprise, and simultaneously maximum likelihood.
The <strong>metric</strong>: perplexity, that loss exponentiated into an effective
branching factor. The <strong>leash</strong>: a KL penalty holding a tuned policy near its
reference, its direction chosen for the asymmetry this module explained. Three things
formerly accepted on faith, now owned.

<section id="summary" class="opening-narrative">
  <h2 id="module-summary">Module Summary</h2>
  <p>
    Surprise is −log p, forced by requiring independent surprises to add; its unit is the
    bit, the surprise of a fair coin. Entropy is expected surprise and, by the source coding
    theorem, the compression floor — a real code's average length sits just above it and no
    code goes below. Cross-entropy is expected surprise under the wrong model: the bits paid
    to encode p's data with q's code, minimised exactly at q = p, which is why it is the
    language-modelling loss and why minimising it is maximum likelihood. KL divergence is
    the excess alone — non-negative by Jensen, asymmetric in ways that do design work, and
    deployed as the leash in preference tuning. Perplexity is cross-entropy exponentiated
    into an effective branching factor: interpretable within one tokenizer, treacherous
    across two. The track closes with its promises kept — and the entropy you can now derive
    as expected surprise will be waiting, relabelled as a storage floor, when the systems
    track begins.
  </p>
