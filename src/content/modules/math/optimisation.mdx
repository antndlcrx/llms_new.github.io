## Loss Surfaces

Start with the object the whole module lives on. A model's parameters are numbers — M1
taught you to see a list of numbers as a <em>point</em>. So every possible setting of a
model's parameters is a point in parameter space, and attaching to each point the loss that
those parameters achieve turns the space into a <em>landscape</em>: parameters as
coordinates, loss as height. Training is finding low ground.

For a two-parameter model you can draw this — height over a plane — and this module's widget
does. A regression's squared-error surface is a smooth bowl with one bottom. A neural
network's surface is nothing of the kind: ridges, multiple valleys of different depths,
plateaus, and — the shape that will matter most — <strong>saddle points</strong>, where the
ground is flat but curves down in one direction and up in another.

One warning, posted early and deliberately: <strong>the 2D picture is a crutch with a stated
expiry.</strong> Real models have millions to billions of parameters, and M1's lesson about
high dimensions applies with force — geometry you cannot picture behaves unlike geometry you
can. The most consequential difference: in high dimensions, critical points where the ground
is flat are overwhelmingly saddles, not minima (a flat point must curve <em>up</em> in every
one of a million directions to be a minimum, and that unanimity is rare). Use the picture;
do not trust it past its expiry.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    Parameters are coordinates, loss is height, training is descending the landscape.
    Regression's landscape is a bowl with a formula for its bottom; a network's landscape has
    no formula, many valleys, and — in high dimensions — flat points that are almost always
    saddles rather than minima.
  </p>
</div>

## Gradient Descent

M5 built the tool this module runs on: the gradient, the direction of steepest ascent, with
its length measuring the slope. Standing anywhere on the loss surface, you therefore know
exactly one useful local fact — which way is most sharply <em>up</em>. The algorithm writes
itself:

Three properties deserve to be stated plainly. The negative sign is there because the
gradient points uphill and we want down — that is the entire justification. The procedure is
<em>greedy</em>: each step consults only the slope underfoot, never the map; nothing prevents
it from marching into a shallow valley while a deeper one sits a ridge away. And it
<em>terminates</em> where the gradient vanishes — which, by M5's definition, includes minima,
maxima (unreachable going downhill), plateaus, and saddles alike. "The gradient is zero" and
"we found the best parameters" are different claims, and conflating them is the misconception
this module exists to retire.

## The Learning Rate

One knob controls the character of everything the previous section described. Watch it work
before theorising about it:

<DescentLandscape />

The behaviours you can produce with that slider are the module's core practical content, and
each has a recognisable signature. <strong>Too small</strong>: every step is safe and
correct, and the run crawls — correct but slow, the least dangerous failure.
<strong>Too large</strong>: each step overshoots the low ground it aimed for, landing on the
far wall; the trajectory zigzags, and past a sharp threshold each overshoot lands
<em>higher</em> than it started and the loss explodes — divergence. <strong>Well
chosen</strong>: a band, not a point — fast progress that settles cleanly. The simplest loss
makes all of this exact:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.quadStep} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.quadStepCaption} />
</div>

Read the factor: for $\eta$ below $1/a$ it shrinks the error smoothly; between $1/a$ and
$2/a$ it is negative — overshooting every step, oscillating — but still shrinking; past $2/a$
its magnitude exceeds one and every step amplifies. <strong>Curvature sets the ceiling:</strong>
the sharper the valley (larger $a$), the smaller the survivable learning rate. That is why
the ravine surface — gentle curvature along the floor, brutal curvature across it — is the
hard case: no single step size suits both directions, so the safe rate for the walls wastes
the floor, and a rate that moves along the floor explodes against the walls. Run the
Oscillate and Diverge presets and watch the same surface produce both behaviours from a 25%
change in one number.

The honest summary for practice: the learning rate is the single most consequential
hyperparameter in deep learning, its safe range is set by the sharpest curvature anywhere on
the path, and its failure modes are <em>visible</em> — a lesson the deep-learning track's
training-dynamics module (D5) turns into a diagnostic skill for real loss curves.

## Stochastic Gradient Descent

The loss surface training actually descends is an average over the whole dataset — and
computing the true gradient means running every one of possibly millions of examples through
the model for every single step. Unaffordable. The fix is the oldest idea in your
discipline: <em>sample</em>.

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.sgdUpdate} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.sgdUpdateCaption} />
</div>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Stochastic Gradient Descent</p>
  <Tex tex={math.sgdDef} />
</div>

A minibatch is a survey sample of the dataset, and everything you know about survey samples
transfers: the estimate is unbiased, its noise shrinks with the square root of the sample
size, and a shockingly small sample is enough when you only need to be right on average.
Descent tolerates per-step error unusually well, because thousands of steps give the errors
room to cancel — the trajectory wobbles around the true descent path rather than departing
from it.

And the noise is not merely tolerated — <strong>it is put to work</strong>. A saddle stalls
exact gradient descent: the gradient underfoot is zero, and a deterministic walker with no
gradient stands still. A stochastic walker never has exactly zero gradient — each minibatch
disagrees slightly — and any nudge with a component along the saddle's downhill direction
gets amplified by the geometry itself. Noise tips the walker off the balance point. The
batch-size dial is therefore a genuine trade: larger batches buy straighter trajectories and
better hardware efficiency at proportionally more compute and memory per step (the systems
track prices this exactly), while smaller batches are cheap, noisy, and — within reason —
usefully noisy.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    The minibatch gradient is a survey estimate of the true gradient — unbiased, noise
    shrinking with the root of the batch size. Descent only needs to be right on average, and
    the noise even helps, tipping the walk off saddles that would stall an exact method.
  </p>
</div>

## Momentum and Adam

Two refinements run nearly every real training job, and both are intuitions you can now own
in one paragraph each — derivations live in the appendix.

<strong>Momentum</strong> treats the trajectory like a heavy ball rather than a drunken walk:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.momUpdate} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.momUpdateCaption} />
</div>

The velocity accumulates gradients over time. Where successive gradients agree — along a
ravine's floor — they compound, and the ball accelerates. Where they alternate — across the
ravine's walls — they cancel, and the zigzag damps itself out. Run the ravine preset with
and without the momentum toggle: the same learning rate that oscillates painfully without it
glides with it. (The heavy-ball analogy is genuinely useful and breaks in instructive
places — problem 5 makes you find them.)

<strong>Adam</strong>, the default optimiser of modern deep learning, adds one more idea:
give <em>every parameter its own effective learning rate</em>, scaled down where gradients
run persistently large and up where they run small — a per-coordinate answer to the ravine
problem, which is after all the situation where different directions demand different step
sizes. Stated plainly, as the spec for this module requires: Adam is the default because it
is robust — it works acceptably across a wide range of problems without tuning — and its
mechanics are fully understood while its interaction with generalisation is genuinely not.
"Everyone uses it and the theory is catching up" is the honest status.

## Convex, Non-Convex, and Why It Works Anyway

Here is what the guarantees you gave up were worth, and the honest state of why losing them
turned out to be survivable.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Convex Function</p>
  <Tex tex={math.convexDef} />
</div>

Convexity is the property that makes optimisation a solved problem: one basin, every local
minimum global, provable convergence at known rates. The classical statistical toolkit lives
almost entirely inside it — which is why "the regression found <em>the</em> estimates" is a
sentence your training permits. Toggle the widget between the convex bowl and the two-well
surface and the difference is visible: on the bowl, every start point and any sane learning
rate reach the same bottom; on the wells, the destination depends on where you start, and
one of the two valleys is simply better than the other.

Neural network losses are emphatically non-convex — composition of nonlinear layers
guarantees it — so on paper, training should be hostage to its starting point, prone to bad
valleys, and unprovable. In practice it works, robustly, at every scale the field has
reached. The current understanding of why, stated with the confidence it deserves — which is
partial: <strong>overparameterisation</strong> means modern networks have vastly more
parameters than strictly needed, and with that surplus the landscape appears to grow many
minima of near-equal quality, so which valley you land in matters less;
<strong>the bad critical points are mostly saddles</strong> in high dimensions, and
stochastic noise escapes saddles; and empirically, <strong>the minima found this way
generalise</strong> — with the relationship between the landscape's geometry and
generalisation an active research area, not a settled story. This is an honest "partly
empirical": the field can describe what happens far better than it can prove why. That
modelling of uncertainty is worth more to a researcher than a tidy false confidence.

Where this lands: training a transformer — the process the LLMs track budgets, the systems
track prices, and the deep-learning track instruments — is exactly this module's procedure,
scaled up: minibatch gradients through M5's chain rule, an Adam step, a learning-rate
schedule, repeated a few hundred thousand times. The single most useful artefact it produces
along the way is the <strong>loss curve</strong> — and you now possess the vocabulary it is
written in: crawls, zigzags, explosions, plateaus, and honest descents.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    Convexity would buy provable, start-independent convergence; networks do not have it.
    Training works anyway — overparameterisation, saddle-escaping noise, many good-enough
    minima — and the full "why" is genuinely open. The loss curve is the instrument, and this
    module taught you to read its handwriting.
  </p>
</div>

    by curvature, and ravines — mismatched curvatures — make any single rate a compromise.
    Minibatches turn the exact gradient into a survey estimate: unbiased, root-of-batch-size
    noise, cheap — and the noise pays for itself by tipping the walk off saddles. Momentum
    accumulates agreement and cancels zigzag; Adam gives each parameter its own step size and
    is the honest, imperfectly-understood default. Convexity would have guaranteed
    everything; networks are not convex, and training works anyway for reasons —
    overparameterisation, saddle-dominance, many good minima — that are partly established
    and partly open. Every training run in this curriculum is this module executed at scale,
    and its loss curve speaks a language of crawls, zigzags, and explosions you can now read.
    The debt planted in M5 — "what does the learning rate control?" — is paid; the next
    debts fall due in D5, where these signatures appear in real training runs.
  </p>
