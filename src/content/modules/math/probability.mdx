## A Distribution Over Fifty Thousand Outcomes

Start with the reframe, stated plainly, because your training points the other way. In a
statistics course, "distribution" summons a bell curve: a continuous variable, a mean, a
standard deviation, tails. The distribution at the heart of a language model is none of
these. It is a <em>list</em> — one entry per token in a vocabulary of roughly fifty
thousand — of non-negative numbers that sum to one. No ordering (token 17,204 is not
"between" its neighbours in any meaningful sense), no distances, no bell.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Categorical Distribution</p>
  <Tex tex={math.catDef} />
</div>

You have met this object before — it is the distribution of a survey question with fifty
thousand response options. Everything you know about discrete probabilities transfers:
probabilities of disjoint outcomes add, the whole list sums to one, expectations are
probability-weighted averages. What changes is only the <em>size</em> and the <em>role</em>:
this distribution is not something you estimate once and report — it is something the model
produces fresh at every position in every sentence, as its answer to the only question it is
ever asked: <em>what comes next?</em>

    The native distribution of language modelling is categorical: a labelled list of
    non-negative numbers summing to one, over a vocabulary of tokens. Not continuous, not
    Gaussian — and produced anew at every position, not estimated once.

## Joint, Marginal, Conditional — and the Chain Rule

The grammar of probability has three moves, and you know all of them from cross-tabulations.

<em>pair</em>. A <strong>marginal</strong> sums out what you do not care about — row and
column totals. And a <strong>conditional</strong> restricts to the rows where something

Sit with this line; it deserves the weight. The probability of an entire text is a product

## Independence, and Why Language Refuses It

Your statistics training prizes independence — it is the assumption that makes everything
tractable. So it is worth seeing exactly what independence <em>costs</em> when the data is
language. The classical bag-of-words model assumes words are independent given a topic:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.bowIndep} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.bowIndepCaption} />
</div>

A product of per-word factors is invariant to reordering its factors — multiplication does
not care. So under bag-of-words, <em>"the government blamed the protesters"</em> and
<em>"the protesters blamed the government"</em> are the same document: same words, same
counts, same probability. For topic detection that is often fine, which is why the LLMs
track's classical-methods module still teaches these models with respect. For meaning it is
fatal — who blamed whom is the content, and it lives entirely in the ordering.

Conditional dependence is not a nuisance to be assumed away; <strong>it is the signal.</strong>
The conditional diverging from the marginal — the next-word distribution <em>given the
prefix</em> differing from the next-word distribution in general — is precisely what it
means for context to inform the next word. The chain rule
never assumed independence, and that refusal is where all of a language model's capacity
lives. The price is that the conditionals are hard to estimate — fifty thousand outcomes,
conditioned on an unbounded prefix — and the transformer is the current best answer to that
estimation problem.

## From Scores to Probabilities: Softmax

A model's final layer produces a vector of raw scores — <em>logits</em> — one per vocabulary
token: arbitrary reals, positive and negative, on no particular scale (M2's linear layer,
one row per token). The chain rule needs a categorical distribution. The bridge is softmax,
and it should be <em>derived</em>, not accepted.

What must the bridge do? Turn any real vector into strictly positive numbers summing to one,
while preserving the ranking of the scores. Normalising raw scores fails immediately —
negative scores yield negative "probabilities" (the problem set makes you find the second,
subtler failure). Exponentiation is the canonical repair: $e^z$ is positive for every real
$z$ and strictly increasing, so exponentiate first, <em>then</em> normalise:

Second, <strong>temperature</strong> — divide all logits by $T$ before exponentiating. This

    log-probability of every next token in the corpus. Cross-entropy loss is this, renamed;
    M8 owns the proof.
  </p>
</div>

## Where This Lands: The √d Debt, Paid

Assemble the module. A language model is a machine for conditional categorical
distributions: feed it a prefix, receive a categorical distribution over the vocabulary.
<strong>Generation is sampling</strong> from that distribution — at temperature, using
exactly the softmax of this module — then appending the sampled token and asking again. The
mysterious fluency of generated text is the chain rule run forwards, fifty thousand-way
categorical draws chained one conditioning step at a time.

And now the promise. M1 defined the attention score as a scaled dot product and flagged the
scale as a question this track would answer honestly rather than wave at: <em>why divide by
the square root of the key dimension?</em> You now hold both required tools — the
high-dimensional geometry of M1 and, as of this module, variance. Query and key vectors have
roughly independent, roughly unit-variance entries (initialisation and normalisation layers
keep them so). Their dot product is a sum of $d$ independent products:

So <em>unscaled</em> scores grow like $\sqrt d$ — at $d_k = 4096$, typical scores near
$\pm 64$. And you have just spent a section learning exactly what score gaps that large do
to a softmax: they are the $T \to 0$ regime arrived at by accident. The distribution
saturates to a near-one-hot; one position monopolises the attention; gradients through every
other position vanish; the network stops learning. The repair is the one your statistics
training reaches for instinctively — <strong>standardise</strong>:

The √d is a z-score. Attention divides by the standard deviation of its own scores so that
the softmax downstream operates in its responsive regime at any model width — the same
normalisation reflex you apply to survey scales, deployed inside the transformer. M1's
question, planted in the track's first module, is closed by its last tool: variance.

  <h2 id="module-summary">Module Summary</h2>
  <p>
    The reframe: probability as generation rather than inference. The native object is the
    categorical distribution — a labelled list summing to one, over fifty thousand tokens —
    produced fresh at every position. The chain rule factors any sequence's probability
    exactly into next-token conditionals, and that single line is what a language model is;
    independence would make the factors easy and the model worthless, because conditional
    dependence is the entire content of context. Softmax is the forced bridge from scores to
    distributions — positive, normalised, rank-preserving, shift-invariant, with temperature
    as sharpness on a fixed ranking rather than confidence. Training is maximum likelihood:
    the summed log-probability of every next token, the same estimator that fitted your
    logit models. And the track's oldest debt is paid: attention divides by √d because
    variances of independent products add — raw dot-product scores spread like √d, which
    would saturate softmax into its unlearnable regime, so the scale is a z-score applied
    where you cannot see it. One handoff remains open: cross-entropy loss "is" maximum
    likelihood, and its unit is the bit. M8 begins there.
  </p>
