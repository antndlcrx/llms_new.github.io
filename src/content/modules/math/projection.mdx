## The Closest Point

Start with a question simple enough to draw. Here is a vector <em>a</em>, and here is a line
through the origin — the span of some vector <em>b</em>. Of all the points on the line,
<strong>which one is closest to <em>a</em></strong>?

Intuition answers immediately: drop a perpendicular from the tip of <em>a</em> onto the line;
where it lands is the closest point. You met that dropped perpendicular in M1 as the dot
product's third reading — the shadow. What M1 did not do is <em>derive</em> it, and with the
dot product in hand the derivation is three lines. Every candidate point on the line has the
form <em>t</em><strong>b</strong> for some number <em>t</em>; ask for the <em>t</em> that
makes the leftover arrow perpendicular to the line:

## Why the Residual Must Be Orthogonal

The derivation <em>assumed</em> the best residual is perpendicular. That assumption deserves
one honest minute, because it is the hinge of the whole module.

  <p>
    Suppose someone proposes a "closest point" whose residual is <em>not</em> orthogonal to
    the line — it leans, say, slightly forwards along the line's direction. Without any
    formulas: can their point really be the closest? What small move improves it?
  </p>
  <details>
    <summary>Reveal</summary>
    <div>
      <p>
        It cannot be closest. A leaning residual has a component <em>along</em> the line, and
        that component is error you can still remove: slide the candidate point along the
        line in the direction of the lean, and the residual shortens — its along-the-line
        part shrinks while its perpendicular part is untouched. Improvement is possible
        exactly as long as any lean remains, so at the true minimum, no lean remains.
        "Closest" and "orthogonal residual" are the same condition, approached from two
        sides. In the sandbox: nudge the line handle slightly and watch the residual length
        rise from its minimum — you are watching this argument run.
      </p>
    </div>
  </details>
</div>

This equivalence — <em>closest means orthogonal</em> — is worth engraving, because everything
downstream is an application of it. Written as an equation, "the residual is orthogonal to
what I projected onto" <em>is</em> the normal equation; you solved it a moment ago for one
line, and in two sections' time you will watch your statistics software solve it for many
columns at once. Same sentence, bigger subspace.

  <p>
    Closest and orthogonal are one condition: while any component of the residual lies along
    the subspace, sliding within the subspace still improves the fit. The normal equations
    are this sentence, written with dot products.

## More Than One Direction: Subspaces

A line is a one-direction subspace. Real problems project onto richer targets: the set of
everything reachable by combining several directions — in M2's language, a column space.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Column Space</p>
  <Tex tex={math.colSpaceDef} />
</div>

The closest-point question survives unchanged: given <strong>y</strong> and a subspace, find
the point of the subspace nearest to it. And the answer keeps its shape: the best point is
the one whose residual is orthogonal to <em>every</em> direction in the subspace — one
orthogonality condition per direction, all holding simultaneously.

One practical subtlety earns a mention. If the directions spanning your subspace are
themselves orthonormal — mutually perpendicular unit vectors — projection is bliss: project
onto each direction separately with the one-line formula and add the results; no interaction,
no system to solve. Non-orthogonal spanning directions overlap and double-count, forcing you
to solve simultaneous equations instead. This is why so much of numerical linear algebra is a
campaign to orthonormalise first — and the appendix's Gram–Schmidt procedure is exactly that
campaign, run with this module's only operation: project, and subtract.

## OLS Is Projection

Now the payoff. Take a regression: an outcome vector <strong>y</strong> holding one number
per observation, and a design matrix <em>X</em> whose columns are your regressors — each
column also one number per observation. Read it geometrically, with M2's eyes: every
candidate coefficient vector picks out a point <em>X</em><strong>β</strong> of the column
space, and the fitted values your software reports are one particular such point. Which one?
The one minimising the sum of squared residuals — which is squared Euclidean
<em>distance</em> — which is to say: <strong>the closest point of the column space to
y</strong>. OLS is projection. It always was.

Every phrase of regression folklore now has a picture. <em>Fitted values</em>: the shadow of
the outcome on the reachable set. <em>Residuals uncorrelated with predictors</em>: the
residual is orthogonal to every column — the normal equations, verbatim (problem 3 makes the
translation exact, intercept and all). <em>The hat matrix "puts the hat on y"</em>: it is a
projection matrix, and the appendix shows its defining properties are geometric platitudes —
projecting twice is projecting once, and orthogonal projection is symmetric. Even
<em>R²</em> is Pythagoras: the squared length of the centred outcome splits into a fitted
part plus a residual part <em>because those two parts are perpendicular</em>.

You have computed all of this many times without being shown it. That is not a criticism of
your training — it is the reason this module sits where it does: the geometry was waiting
inside a method you already trust, and from here the track can spend that trust elsewhere.

## Semantic Projection as Measurement

The same operation, pointed at language. M1 established that in an embedding space,
<em>relations are directions</em> — the arrow from <em>he</em> to <em>she</em> carries
something like gender. Projection is what turns that observation into an instrument:

  <Tex tex={math.axisFormula} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.axisFormulaCaption} />
</div>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Semantic Axis</p>
  <Tex tex={math.axisDef} />
</div>

Project <em>nurse</em>, <em>engineer</em>, <em>banker</em> onto a gender axis and each
receives a coordinate; project onto a rich–poor axis and each receives another. Kozlowski,
Taddy and Evans used exactly this to trace the cultural meaning of class across a century of
text; Grand and colleagues showed such projections recover fine-grained human judgements
about objects. It is a genuinely elegant method — one derivation, no training, interpretable
by construction.

Because it is a measurement instrument, its assumptions must be stated as sharply as its
formula. <strong>It assumes the contrast is linear</strong> — that "gender" is a single
straight direction rather than a curved or context-dependent structure. <strong>It assumes
the pairs isolate one dimension</strong> — but <em>king–queen</em> drags royalty along with
gender, and the axis inherits every such stowaway. <strong>It assumes the corpus defines the
semantics</strong> — the axis measures the training text's usage, so a coordinate for
<em>nurse</em> is a fact about written language as sampled, not about nurses. None of these
are reasons not to use the method; all of them are reasons to validate it — many pairs,
sensitivity checks, external ground truth — with exactly the discipline the LLMs track's
validation module demands of any classifier.
