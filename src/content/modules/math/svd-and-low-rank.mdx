## Directions a Matrix Only Stretches

Go back to the M2 canvas for a moment. Under most matrices, most vectors <em>rotate</em>:
they come out pointing somewhere new. But watch closely and some matrices have special
directions that refuse to turn — vectors that come out longer or shorter or flipped, but
still on their own line. Try it: set the M2 widget to the Scale preset, and notice the two
axes pass through unrotated while every diagonal direction swings.

Those unturning directions are the <strong>eigenvectors</strong>, and the stretch factors
are the <strong>eigenvalues</strong>:

  <Tex tex={math.eigenEq} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.eigenEqCaption} />
</div>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Eigenvector, Eigenvalue</p>
  <Tex tex={math.eigenDef} />
</div>

Why care about the directions a matrix treats simply? Because along them, a complicated
transformation reduces to multiplication by a number — and if you can find enough of them
to form a basis, the <em>whole</em> matrix reduces to numbers along special axes. That is
the decomposition programme this module runs on: take an opaque matrix apart into rotations
(which you understand) and per-axis scalings (which you understand), with nothing left over.

One honest caution before the programme succeeds: it does not always work with eigenvectors
alone. A rotation matrix turns <em>every</em> direction — no real eigenvectors at all
(problem 1 makes you meet this). The general tool needs one more idea, and it arrives in
section three.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    An eigenvector is a direction a matrix does not rotate — only stretches, by its
    eigenvalue. Along eigenvectors a matrix acts like plain multiplication, which is why
    finding them is the first step of taking a matrix apart.
  </p>
</div>

## The Symmetric Special Case

For one family of matrices, the programme works perfectly — and it happens to be the family
your data already lives in. A <strong>symmetric</strong> matrix (equal to its own transpose)
always has a complete set of eigenvectors, they are mutually <em>orthogonal</em>, and every
eigenvalue is real:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.symFact} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.symFactCaption} />
</div>

M2 promised that a symmetric matrix is "a pure scale along some rotated set of axes"; the
spectral theorem is that promise with a proof (sketched in the appendix). Rotate into the
right frame — the orthogonal eigenvectors — and the matrix is diagonal: nothing but
independent per-axis stretches. All the complexity was in <em>which axes</em>.

And symmetric matrices are everywhere in this curriculum, not as a curiosity but by
construction: every <strong>covariance matrix</strong> is symmetric (the covariance of
question 3 with question 7 is the covariance of 7 with 3); every <strong>Gram matrix</strong>
of pairwise dot products is symmetric (M1: the dot product does not care about order); the
matrix of <strong>attention scores</strong> before masking is built from exactly such
products. When you meet a symmetric matrix, you now know its anatomy on sight: orthogonal
axes, real stretches, no shear, no rotation left over.

## The SVD: Every Matrix, Taken Apart

The eigenvector programme fails for rotations and cannot even be posed for non-square
matrices — and your data matrices are rarely square. The repair is one of the great moves
in mathematics: stop demanding the <em>same</em> direction in and out. Ask instead for an
orthogonal set of input directions that map to an orthogonal set of output directions.
That request is granted for <strong>every matrix, without exception</strong>:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.svdEq} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.svdEqCaption} />
</div>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Rank-1 Matrix</p>
  <Tex tex={math.rankOne} />
</div>

Read the sum form the way you read a regression table. Each term is a rank-1 atom: it
listens for one input direction (v) and speaks along one output direction (u) — the
simplest possible matrix, the M2 Collapse preset dignified with a job. The singular value
on the front says <em>how loudly</em> that channel speaks. And the channels come sorted:
the first term is the single best rank-1 account of the whole matrix, the second the best
correction to it, and so on down a diminishing tail. A matrix, on this reading, is not a
grid of numbers — it is a ranked list of stories, most important first.

The misconception this module fixes is now visible: <em>the SVD is not "a function you call
to reduce dimensions".</em> It is a statement about what a matrix <em>is</em> — a sum of
importance-weighted rank-1 pieces. Dimension reduction is merely what happens when you stop
reading the list early.

## PCA, Which You Already Do

You have run this module's machinery before, under an assumed name. Principal component
analysis — the workhorse behind factor analysis, scale construction, and every
"two-dimensional map of our respondents" plot — <em>is</em> the SVD, applied to centred
data:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.pcaEq} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.pcaEqCaption} />
</div>

The pieces assemble from earlier modules. Centre the data — M1's centering, moving the
cloud's average to the origin, which is what makes "directions of spread" meaningful.
Decompose. The right singular vectors are the principal components: the orthogonal
directions along which your respondents actually differ, importance-ordered by explained
variance. A hundred-question battery whose top three components explain most of the
variance is a hundred-column matrix that is, for all practical purposes, rank three — the
survey measures three things, asked a hundred ways. In this curriculum PCA earns its keep
visualising embedding spaces: project 768 dimensions onto the top two components and look.

## Where This Lands: The Low-Rank Bet

Here is the payoff the whole module has been arranging. Fine-tuning a language model means
updating its weight matrices — and a single attention projection in a mid-sized model is
that 4096 × 4096 matrix from the widget's arithmetic: 16.8 million parameters, times dozens
of layers. Updating all of it, for every task, is the brute-force option.

The alternative rests on one hypothesis:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.loraEq} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.loraEqCaption} />
</div>

Notice precisely what is being assumed — problem 6 has you argue it properly. Not that the
weight matrix is low-rank: pretrained weights genuinely use their dimensions. The bet is
that the <em>update</em> — what a specific task needs to change — is low-rank: a new domain
emphasis, an output format, a register, expressible in a few directions of adjustment. On
that bet, the 256× arithmetic you just computed becomes the training budget: freeze the
16.8 million, learn 65 thousand. You have been using this technique's result since the
fine-tuning module of the LLMs course; its name is LoRA, and when L10 teaches it as an
engineering practice — ranks, targets, quantised variants — the entire conceptual content
will already be yours: <em>it is the rank slider, applied to a change in weights.</em>

    matrices — betting that what a task changes is low-rank even though the weights are
    not. The rank slider is the whole idea; L10 supplies the engineering.
  </p>
</div>

  <h2 id="module-summary">Module Summary</h2>
  <p>
    An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches, and for symmetric matrices —
    covariance, Gram, pre-mask attention — a full orthogonal set of them exists: the
    spectral theorem, M2's "scale along rotated axes" made rigorous. The SVD extends the
    programme to every matrix by letting input and output directions differ: rotate, scale,
    rotate, or equivalently a sum of rank-1 pieces ordered by importance, which is the form
    worth thinking in. Truncating that sum is the provably optimal low-rank approximation
    (Eckart–Young), its error exactly the discarded spectrum — and structured matrices
    concentrate so much in so few directions that recognisability and meaning survive at
    ranks costing hundreds of times fewer parameters. PCA is this machinery applied to
    centred data, with the standing caution that variance is not interest and the top
    component of embedding spaces is often frequency. And the landing: low-rank adaptation
    bets that fine-tuning <em>updates</em> are low-rank, freezing millions of parameters to
    learn thousands — the rank slider, applied to weight changes, three modules before L10
    gives it its name.
  </p>
