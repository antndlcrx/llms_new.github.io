## What a Vector Is

The misconception this module exists to fix: <strong>a vector is a list of numbers.</strong>
It is not. A vector is a point in a space — or equivalently an arrow from the origin to that
point — and the list of numbers is a <em>description</em> of it, written in a particular
coordinate system. The distinction sounds pedantic and is not: the coordinates are how you
store a vector; the geometry is what the vector <em>means</em>. Until the picture flips from
"list" to "point," every later geometric argument passes through as vocabulary.

You already own the intuition. A survey respondent who answered a ten-question battery is
a point in a ten-dimensional space — one axis per question, their answers as coordinates.
Two respondents with similar politics sit near each other in that space. Nothing was added
to the data by saying this; the geometry was always there. Nobody drew it.

Because a vector is a geometric object, it supports two kinds of manipulation, and being able
to move between them is the first objective. <em>Algebraically</em>, you add vectors
coordinate-by-coordinate and stretch them by multiplying every coordinate by the same number.
<em>Geometrically</em>, addition places arrows head-to-tail, and scalar multiplication stretches
or flips an arrow without changing the line it lies on. Every computation in this track can be
read both ways, and whenever one reading is opaque the other is usually not.

## Length and Normalisation

The first genuinely geometric quantity: how long is a vector?

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.normDef} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.normCaption} />
</div>

The norm is actual length — the straight-line distance from the origin to the point. For a
document represented as word counts, the norm mostly reflects how <em>long the document is</em>:
double the text and every count roughly doubles, so the vector doubles too, pointing the same
way but reaching twice as far.

That observation motivates the module's second tool. To <strong>normalise</strong> a vector is
to divide it by its own norm, producing a <strong>unit vector</strong>: same direction, length
exactly one.

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Unit Vector</p>
  <Tex tex={math.unitDef} />
</div>

Be precise about the trade, because it is a modelling decision and not a formality:
<strong>normalisation throws magnitude away, on purpose.</strong> Whether that is right depends
entirely on what magnitude encodes in your data. For text, magnitude is usually document
length — a nuisance parameter you are glad to lose. For a spending profile, magnitude is how
much was spent — possibly the entire point. The mathematics will not warn you either way;
knowing what the discarded quantity <em>meant</em> is your job, not the formula's.

## The Dot Product

The single most load-bearing operation in this entire curriculum. It has three readings, and
the payload of this section is that they are the same number.

<strong>Reading one — algebraic.</strong> Multiply matching coordinates and sum:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.dotAlg} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.dotAlgCaption} />
</div>

This is the reading computers use — a few multiplications and additions, which is why GPUs can
do trillions of them per second. It is also, on its own, meaningless: staring at the formula
tells you nothing about <em>what the number says</em>.

<strong>Reading two — geometric.</strong> The same number, computed from lengths and the angle:

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.dotGeo} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.dotGeoCaption} />
</div>

<strong>Reading three — projective.</strong> How much of one vector points along the other:
the dot product is the length of the shadow that <em>a</em> casts on <em>b</em>'s direction,
times the length of <em>b</em>. When you drop a perpendicular from the tip of <em>a</em> onto
the line through <em>b</em>, the foot of that perpendicular marks the shadow. This reading is
the one attention needs: it answers <em>"how much does this vector contain of that direction?"</em>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Dot Product</p>
  <Tex tex={math.dotDef} />
</div>

And one property that most treatments skip past, even though downstream mechanisms depend on
it: <strong>the sign carries meaning.</strong> A positive dot product means the vectors are
aligned — less than 90&deg; apart. Exactly zero means <strong>orthogonal</strong>, and for
embeddings the right reading of orthogonal is <em>unrelated</em> — not opposite, simply nothing
to do with each other. Negative means opposed — pointing in contrary directions. Keep the
three-way distinction; it recurs from attention scores to preference optimisation.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    One number, three readings: coordinate arithmetic (what machines compute), lengths times
    the cosine of the angle (what it measures), and projection (how much of one direction is
    inside another). Positive is aligned, zero is unrelated, negative is opposed.
  </p>
</div>

## Cosine Similarity

The dot product entangles two things: how aligned the vectors are, and how long they are.
Sometimes you want exactly that mixture — attention will — but for comparing documents you
usually want alignment alone. The fix is the same move as normalisation: divide the lengths out.

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.cosine} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.cosineCaption} />
</div>

<div class="definition-box">
  <p class="definition-box__label">Definition</p>
  <p class="definition-box__term">Cosine Similarity</p>
  <Tex tex={math.cosineDef} />
</div>

Why is this the standard similarity measure for text? Because of what each measure responds
to. Euclidean distance between two document vectors responds to <em>everything</em> —
direction and magnitude both — and for text, magnitude is length. A tweet and a five-thousand-word
report on the same topic sit far apart in Euclidean terms purely because one is longer. Cosine
ignores that: it asks only whether they point the same way. The same logic runs from the sparse
count vectors of classical text analysis to the dense embeddings of the LLMs track — the
vectors changed, the comparison did not.

Notice also what you get by combining this section with the last: <strong>for unit vectors,
the dot product <em>is</em> the cosine similarity</strong> — the denominators are both 1 and
disappear. This one-line fact is why real systems normalise their embeddings once and then use
plain dot products for search: the cheap operation and the right-invariance operation become
the same operation.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <p class="key-takeaway__label">Key Takeaway</p>
  <p>
    Cosine similarity is the dot product with magnitude divided out — direction-only agreement.
    Text uses it because document length is noise, and for unit vectors it coincides with the
    dot product, which is why embedding pipelines normalise once and dot-product ever after.
  </p>
</div>

## Where This Lands: Attention

Here is where this module's tools point. Inside a transformer, every token position asks a
question of every other position. The question is a vector <strong>q</strong> (a query); each
position's offer is a vector <strong>k</strong> (a key); and the relevance of that offer to
this question is —

<div class="formula-block">
  <Tex tex={math.attnScore} />
  <Tex class="formula-block__caption" tex={math.attnCaption} />
</div>

— a dot product. An attention score measures <em>how much this position's question aligns with
that position's offer</em>, in precisely the sense you now own: projection of one vector onto
another, sign and all. When the course's transformer module walks the full mechanism, this is
the step where the "understanding" happens; the rest is bookkeeping around it.

One honest loose end, flagged rather than glossed: the division by the square root of the key
dimension. It is doing real statistical work, and explaining it requires knowing how the
variance of a sum of random terms grows — machinery that belongs to M7. Hold the question
<em>"why that denominator?"</em>; it will be answered, not waved at.

        "Opposite" is a different geometry: a strongly <em>negative</em> score, the key pointing
        against the query. The distinction matters because the scores next pass through a

  </details>
</div>

    a genuine open question here, and M7 closes it.

  <h2 id="module-summary">Module Summary</h2>
  <p>
    A vector is a point or a direction in a space, and its coordinates are a description, not
    the thing itself — a flip that makes your existing data geometric. The norm measures length;
    normalisation deletes it, keeping pure direction, which for text removes the nuisance of
    document size. The dot product is one number with three readings — coordinate arithmetic,

    comparison text analysis runs on; keep the lengths in and it is the raw material of an
    attention score, the transformer's unit of relevance. One open question was planted on
    purpose: why attention divides by the square root of the key dimension. M7 pays that debt.
  </p>
